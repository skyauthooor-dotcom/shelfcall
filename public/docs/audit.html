What Breaks

Sixteen edge cases the current design cannot survive, the places where the flows and stories now contradict the prototypes, and what the data model needs before you can tell what the system is doing — let alone correct it.

Each case now carries its status. Eleven are built and clickable in the prototype; one is half built and waiting on a server; four are deliberate decisions or jobs too small to reach without the real thing. The drift table below is closed — the spec is v0.3 and matches the build.

01

Orders that get stuck

Every one of these leaves a real book, a real person and sometimes real cash in a state the app has no way out of. They are the most alarming because nobody notices until someone complains.

E1The shop confirms, then sells the book over the counterstuckmoney

A walk-in customer buys the same copy an hour after the shop confirmed the order. The courier arrives to collect nothing, or the reader turns up to an empty counter.

There is no seller cancel after confirming. The reader can only cancel while the order is PLACED. The sub-order is frozen.

Seller can cancel a confirmed order with a reason, up to the moment of handover. Reader notified, request reopens, its released offers come back. Count these per shop — a shop that does it often is overselling.

Built. The reason-first block I cannot do this one is on the seller’s order page through PLACED and READY, and through SHIPPED for an uncollected pickup. Reversal is exact — see E5.

E2The courier cannot deliverstuckmoney

Nobody home, wrong address, phone off, or the buyer refuses on the doorstep. If the shop was paid at pickup, the courier is holding a book and is out of pocket.

Not modelled at all. The job stays in On you forever and the order never completes, so the sale is never credited and the commission never accrues.

A third courier action: could not deliver, with a reason (no answer / refused / wrong address). It returns the book to the shop, cancels the sub-order, and — when the shop was already paid — opens a refund to the courier line so the float reconciles.

Built. Could not deliver, with three reasons, on the job card. The book goes back to the shop, the sub-order cancels, the request reopens, and a shop already paid at pickup opens a refund the office owes the courier.

E3The reader never collects a pickupstuck

The book sits behind the counter, off the shelf, for weeks. The code is never entered, so no sale is recorded and the shop is holding stock for nobody.

No expiry on READY FOR PICKUP. The shop has no way to release it and no way to tell the app it gave up.

A pickup window — seven days is a sane default, warned at day five. Then the shop can release it in one tap: sub-order cancelled, book back on the shelf, request reopened. Record it as reader-side non-collection, not a seller fault.

Built, shorter. Three days, not seven — a book off the shelf for a week is a week of dead stock. The shop releases it in one tap and the copy says whether the window has passed. The state is derived from ready_at; the reminder itself is a scheduled task.

E4Nothing actually runs the clocksstucksystemic

The design leans on a dozen timed transitions: the confirm nudge and the auto-cancel after it, the 24 h / 72 h / 30-day silence ladder, each shop’s statement on its own anniversary day, restriction that evening — plus the pickup window above.

None of them are specified as jobs. In the prototypes they are computed on render, which hides the problem: in a real system, if the job does not fire, the state never changes and nobody is told.

One scheduled-jobs table with name, schedule, last_run_at, last_error, rows_touched. If a job has not run today, that is visible on the ops page. Without this, a silent cron failure looks exactly like a quiet week.

Half built. The rule is settled and written down: derive for display, queue for consequence. Four of the clocks are read-time queries that cannot silently fail — the 72-hour fallback pool, the 24-hour review edit, the free month and the payment overdue check. The rest produce a consequence and need one ScheduledTask table and one worker: specified in the model, not yet built, because a prototype has no server. The auto-cancel is one of them, and it is now settled as the end of the next open day rather than a count of open hours — a shop that shuts early loses no time it was never open for, and both sides can be told a date instead of watching a counter.

E5Cancelling revives offers that should stay deadcorrectness

Ordering closes every other offer on the request as not chosen. Cancelling the order reopens the request and revives them — including offers the reader had declined earlier, and offers whose seller has since removed the book.

The reversal matches on status alone. An offer has no memory of why it closed.

closed_by_order_id and closed_reason on every offer. Reversal revives only the offers closed by this order, and never one the seller removed in the meantime.

Built. closed_by_order_id and closed_reason on every offer. A cancel revives only what that order closed; a copy the seller removed in the meantime stays dead. Verified by click-through.

E6Two readers order the same physical copytrust

One Frankenstein offered to three requests. Two readers order within the same hour. The same-copy prompt only appears after the first confirmation, which is too late.

The shop must decline one order. Declining is currently framed as a failure and the reader is simply told no.

Keep the prompt, but add the honest release valve: a decline reason of “already sold” that costs the shop nothing, an apology to the reader that reopens the request automatically, and a count watched internally. Optionally let a seller mark an offer as one copy only, so the app can warn at order time rather than after.

Built. The same-copy prompt now fires on a title match only, and names the book. Sold in the shop is the first exit reason, and costs the shop nothing.

E7A location is retired with orders in flightminor

Retiring a branch checks for live offers but not for confirmed sub-orders that name it. The courier is sent to an address the shop has closed.

Block retirement while any sub-order at that location is not finished, and say how many.

Still open. Retirement still checks live offers only. Small, and unreachable in the prototype — do it with the real settings screen.

02

Where the money leaks

Cash plus a manual ledger is workable at ten shops. Each of these is the specific way it stops being workable.

E8Only the courier says the shop was paidmoney

The courier taps “paid the shop” and the ledger clears. The shop is never asked to confirm it received anything.

One-sided record. A disagreement three weeks later has no second source, and the courier is an employee, so the loss is yours either way.

Two-sided settlement: the courier marks it paid, the shop gets a one-tap received on their orders screen. Unmatched pairs older than a day are the ops page's first list.

Built. Two-sided: the courier marks it paid, and the shop gets Yes, I got it / No, I did not on their orders screen. Unmatched pairs are the ops page’s first list.

E9Cash state is stored as booleans, not entriesmoneymodel

Today: paidShop: true, settled: true. Totals are derived by looping and summing.

You cannot answer when, how much, who recorded it, or what changed since yesterday. You cannot correct a mistake without overwriting the only copy of the truth.

A cash ledger of rows — see §05. Every movement is an entry: buyer → courier, courier → shop, courier → office, office → courier (refunds). Corrections are new reversing entries, never edits.

Built. Cash is rows now — {at, amount, from, to, sub, note, pending} — and every figure on both money screens is a sum over them. No paid_shop boolean survives. Money owed and money held are different things: a refund the office promises is pending until someone marks it received.

E10A completed order that went wrong still bills the shopmoneytrust

The book arrives water-damaged, or is the wrong edition. The order is already COMPLETED, so it counts towards the rating, the order count and the statement.

There are no returns in v1, and no state between completed and disputed. The reader's only recourse is a bad review; the shop is charged regardless.

You do not need refunds in v1, but you do need a dispute flag on a completed sub-order that holds its commission line out of the statement until an operator resolves it. The flag already exists on the statement side — give the reader a way to raise it.

Built. The reader can raise a problem on a completed order, reason first. The line is held out of the shop’s statement until an operator resolves it.

E11Restriction does not stop an unpaid shop earningpolicy

A restricted shop keeps every live offer, and those offers can still be ordered for weeks. Restriction only blocks new requests reaching them.

A deliberate choice, not a bug — but decide it explicitly. Letting old offers convert is the humane version and keeps readers served; it also means a shop can ignore a statement for a month with little pain. Consider pausing a restricted shop's offers after a second unpaid statement.

Decided, not built. Old offers keep converting. It is the humane version and it keeps readers served. Revisit if a shop ignores a second statement — not before.

E12A second account is a second free monthpolicy

Nothing ties a shop account to a real shop. The cheapest way to never pay 12% is to re-register every 30 days.

At ten shops you will notice by name. Before you have fifty, tie the account to something re-usable-once: a phone number, a tax id, or an operator's manual approval on first payout.

Still open. At ten shops you notice by name. Tie the account to a phone number or a tax id before fifty.

03

Trust and privacy

E13The buyer's phone and address go out and never come backprivacy

On confirmation the shop sees the reader's name, phone and address; the courier sees them too. If the order is then cancelled or declined, the details have already been handed over.

Accept it for v1 — a courier genuinely needs an address — but state it: details are released at confirmation and cannot be recalled, show the reader that this is what confirmation means, and keep the release stamped in the event log so you can answer a complaint.

Built, and tightened. Details are now released at ready, not at confirm — later than before, and only once a shop has committed a copy. The order page says so rather than showing an empty field. Still irrevocable once out.

E14The reader can complete an order that never happenedmoney

“I have it — mark as received” is available to the reader on any shipped order. Tapping it completes the sale, credits the shop and charges commission, whether or not a book changed hands.

It exists as a fallback for when the shop or courier forgets. But it is a second way to close an order with no counter-signature.

Keep it, and record which party closed it — that field already exists. Then watch the share of orders closed by the reader alone: a healthy system closes most by code or courier.

Built. closed_by records code, courier or reader on every completed sub-order. Watch the reader-only share.

E15A review can be left on an order the seller disputesminor

Reviews unlock on completion and are final after 24 hours, with no seller reply and no moderation path.

Out of scope is fine, but an operator needs the ability to hide a review pending a dispute. That is an ops-page action and a hidden_at field, not a feature.

Still open. An ops field and an ops action, not a feature. Do it when there is something to moderate.

E16Nothing stops an abandoned request filling the feedminor

The 30-day archive only fires on requests with zero offers. A request with three ignored offers stays open forever.

Archive on reader inactivity as well: no visit and no action for 30 days, offers or not. Tell the reader, and let one tap bring it back.

Still open. The archive still needs the offer count at zero. Add reader inactivity when the scheduled-task worker exists — it is a row in the same table.

The prototypes moved faster than the written spec. These are the places a developer reading the spec would build the wrong thing — all six are now closed, and the spec is at v0.3. The table is kept because what was wrong, and how it was settled, is worth reading once.

Stories that do not exist and need to

Recovery

  • Seller cancels after confirming (E1) — reason, notification, request reopens
  • Courier could not deliver (E2) — return to shop, cancel, refund the float
  • Pickup not collected (E3) — window, warning, shop releases it
  • Reader raises a problem on a completed order (E10) — holds the commission line

Operations

  • Scheduled jobs (E4) — what runs, when, and what is visible when one fails
  • Two-sided settlement (E8) — the shop confirms the courier's cash
  • Operator corrections — any state change reversible by hand, and logged
  • Metrics — the supply-test numbers computed from live data rather than a spreadsheet
05

What the data model needs to be steerable

The current model stores the present: a status field and a few booleans. That is enough to render a screen and nothing else. To run the business you need to answer three questions — what happened, why is this stuck, and what changed since yesterday — and to fix a wrong state without destroying the evidence.

The four things missing

1 · An append-only event log

  • Every state change as a row: at, actor_id, actor_role, entity, entity_id, from_state, to_state, reason, source (app | job | operator).
  • This is what turns “why is order 1042 stuck” from guesswork into a query.
  • It is also the only way an operator correction is distinguishable from a user action.

2 · A cash ledger, not booleans

  • One row per movement: amount, from_party, to_party, sub_order_id, at, recorded_by, confirmed_by.
  • Parties: buyer, shop, courier, office.
  • Corrections are reversing entries. Nothing is ever edited.
  • The courier's float, the shop's share and the office's fees all become sums over this table rather than three separate derived numbers that can disagree.

3 · Timestamps and reasons on everything

  • Not status alone: placed_at, confirmed_at, ready_at, collected_at, delivered_at, completed_at, cancelled_at.
  • Every terminal state carries an enum reason. Free text is a note, never the record.
  • closed_by_order_id on offers, so reversal is exact (E5).
  • version on an offer, held by the cart line — “price changed since you added it” becomes precise instead of a comparison of two numbers.

4 · Nothing is ever deleted

  • retired_at on locations, removed_at on offers, hidden_at on reviews.
  • A shop that retires a branch must not orphan the orders and statements that reference it.
  • Soft delete plus the event log is what lets you rebuild any screen as it looked last Tuesday — which is what a disputed statement actually requires.

06

If you only do five things

Ordered by damage prevented per hour of work. The first three are cheap and stop real harm; the last two are the difference between a system you run and one that runs you.

  1. Give every stuck state an exit. Seller cancels after confirming, courier could not deliver, pickup expires. Three actions, each with a reason, each reopening the request. Without them, your first bad week produces orders nobody can close.
  2. Add closed_by_order_id and enum reasons to offers and sub-orders. Small schema change, removes a whole class of wrong-state bugs, and makes every later metric possible.
  3. Make settlement two-sided. The shop taps received. One button, and the cash model stops resting on a single person's memory.
  4. Replace the cash booleans with a ledger table before the first real delivery. Retrofitting a ledger over historical booleans is painful; starting with one costs nothing.
  5. Write the event log from day one, even if nothing reads it yet. It is the cheapest thing on this list to add now and the most expensive to add later, and it is what makes the ops page able to correct rather than just observe.

What I would deliberately not fix yet

  • Returns and refunds — the dispute flag (E10) is enough until someone actually needs money back.
  • Phone masking (E13) — a courier needs a real number; say so plainly instead of building indirection.
  • Review moderation (E15) — one operator field, not a feature.
  • Duplicate-account prevention (E12) — you will recognise ten shops by name.
