Shelfcall

A reverse marketplace for books. Readers describe what they are after — an exact edition or just “something about a crazy scientist” — and booksellers answer with real copies from their shelves, photographed and priced. The reader compares the offers, checks who the seller is, and orders.

What changed

Everything below is built and clickable in the prototype. This list exists so nobody reads a stale paragraph further down and builds the wrong thing.

v0.3 — three doors, one delivery

  • Three different ways in, on purpose. A reader signs up with Google and gives nothing else — name, phone and address are asked at checkout, when there is an order to put them on. A bookseller fills everything in four screens, because a shop with no name, address or hours cannot be offered to anyone. A courier has no sign-up at all: the office issues the account. (US-01, US-02)
  • Delivery is one event for the order. The courier still collects shop by shop, but hands over once, at one door, and takes the cash once. The button is on the order and only appears when every shop has been collected from. (US-35, US-37)
  • The delivery fee is the platform's, not the shop's. One fee per order whatever it holds — two books cost the same as one, two shops the same as one. Booksellers no longer set a courier price; they are paid for books. (US-20, US-39)
  • Auto-cancel at the end of the next open day, replacing the unsettled four-open-hours rule. (US-23)
  • Posting a request opens on the kind of asking — a book you can name, or a book you are describing — each with a sentence and a real example, because the two lead to different forms and different offers. (US-04)
  • The offer note is never required, on any request. Two things are required of a book: what it is and what it costs. (US-11)
  • Books in an offer stay editable until it is sent, back reaches the previous book, and Send opens whichever book is short of something. (US-12)
  • Orders split into On the way and Finished for readers and booksellers alike. (US-25)
  • The feed is only what you have not answered. Sending an offer takes the request out of the feed and into My offers; it can still be answered again from there. (US-08, US-09, S-12)
  • Booksellers have a photo, with initials as the fallback — never a stock shopfront. (US-02)
  • Every touch target reaches 36px and no screen scrolls sideways; measured across all 29 screens, not judged by eye.

v0.2 — the exits and the ledger

  • No “confirm”. The bookseller's one action is Ready for delivery or Ready for pickup, pressed with the book in hand. The CONFIRMED state is gone from both delivery paths. (US-23, §04)
  • Orders open as pages. Nothing on the seller's order list acts; a decision that sends a courier across town should not be one thumb-flick from a scroll. (S-16, S-16b)
  • The exit stays open after ready. A book that breaks overnight can be pulled before the courier travels, reason first. (US-24)
  • Checkout is one screen. Cart → checkout → placed. Returning readers see their details summarised with an Edit; first-timers see the form. (US-21, S-08)
  • A note for the courier is captured at checkout and shown on the job. (US-21, US-35)
  • The offer note leads the form and the card on an open-ended request, with a screen of its own — but it is never required. (US-11, US-15)
  • One offer, several books. Books are composed into a basket and sent together. (US-12)
  • Courier jobs group by order, with a card per bookshop inside. One trip, one address, several stops. (US-35, S-21)
  • The courier's history is shop by shop, with a pay button per outstanding job. (US-38, S-23)
  • Light, dark, or match the phone — a setting in every role's profile. (US-03)
  • Errors are red, as a token, so they read in both themes.
  • Delivery is chosen once for the order, not per bookseller, and pickup is only on the table when every shop in the cart does pickup. (US-20)
  • No form is taller than a phone screen. Every screen that asks for input is short enough that its button sits above the fold on a 390×844 phone once the browser's own chrome is taken off — measured, not estimated. Posting a request is two steps; making an offer is two, or three when the request is open-ended and the note earns a screen of its own. Optional fields live on their own optional screen rather than lengthening a required one.
  • The note for the courier is the courier's. It is captured at checkout, shown to the reader on their own order and to the courier on the job, and never to the bookseller — the field says so where it is typed.
  • The design system is shadcn/ui, adopted whole: their default Neutral palette in OKLCH, their 0.625rem radius scale, their component anatomy and their data-slot markup. Geist and Geist Mono for type, which is shadcn's own choice. Nothing is a Shelfcall variant of a shadcn value. Four components have no shadcn equivalent and are ours: BookCover, HandoverCode, ReasonBlock and OfferCard.
  • Two contrast failures in shadcn's own defaults are kept exact rather than retinted, each with a documented workaround: the light-theme focus ring at 2.6:1 (answered with a 3px ring), and white on destructive in dark at 2.9:1 (answered by flipping the ink).

Model additions

  • CashEntry — the whole cash position as rows, with a pending flag for money owed but not yet handed over. No paid_shop boolean anywhere.
  • ScheduledTask — one table, one worker, for everything that must happen at a time.
  • EventLog — append-only; the answer to “who cancelled this and why”.
  • Offer.closed_by_order_id and closed_reason — so a cancellation revives exactly the offers that order closed, and no others.
  • SubOrder.ready_at, cancelled_by, cancel_reason.
  • Order.courier_note; User.theme; the reader's default courier note.

Still open

  • Auto-cancel at four open hours, or at the end of the next open day? Settled: the end of the next open day. A shop that shuts early loses no time it was never open for, and the reader still gets a date rather than a countdown.
  • Where the first thirty readers come from. Google sign-in answers how someone registers, not why they turn up. This is the one unanswered question that can sink the thing.
  • Review moderation: an ops field, not a feature, until there is something to moderate.
  • Duplicate accounts and a restricted seller's old offers — both left deliberately unhandled in v1.
01

Who it is for

Two roles, one loop. Everything in this MVP exists to make that loop complete once, end to end, and to make it worth repeating.

Reader / Buyer

Dina, hunts by mood

Reads two or three books a month. Half the time she knows the exact title; the other half she only knows the feeling she wants.

  • Wants to describe a book in her own words, not fight a search box.
  • Judges an offer by the photo, the condition and who the seller is.
  • Will buy from two different sellers in one go if the basket is easy.
  • Fails at: keyword search, ISBN lookups, browsing endless catalogues.
Bookseller

Aram, second-hand shop

Has 4,000 books he will never catalogue. Checks his phone between customers.

  • Wants a feed of live demand, newest first, that he can skim in a minute.
  • Answers with a photo from the shelf, a price and two lines of description.
  • Competes on presentation and reputation, not on being first in a search index.
  • Fails at: uploading an inventory, maintaining stock counts, writing listings.
Courier

Vahe, on a scooter

On the payroll — our courier, not a contractor. Carries books across Yerevan and never reads the app for pleasure: he opens it, does the job, closes it.

  • Needs two addresses, a phone number and one figure: the cash to collect.
  • Two taps in his whole day: collected, delivered.
  • Holds the buyer's cash until he settles with the shop.
  • Fails at: reading, choosing, anything with more than one decision on screen.
Demand comes firstNothing is listed until someone asks. The request is the unit of the product; the offer is the answer.
Both request kinds are first class“Bulgakov, Master and Margarita, 1988 edition” and “a book about a crazy scientist” go through the same form and the same feed.
Cards, not conversationsThere is no messaging. Everything a buyer needs to decide must fit on the offer card and the seller profile.
Reputation is the trust layerRating, completed orders and buyer comments travel with the seller onto every offer card.
One cart, many shopsA single checkout splits into one sub-order per seller location, each fulfilled and paid independently.
Every copy has an addressA bookseller declares their locations and says which one each book is on, so a reader always knows where the copy physically is.
Offers don’t rotNothing expires. An offer stays live until the reader orders — then it, and every other offer on that request, is done. Until then the seller can edit every field or remove it outright.
Money never touches the platformThe reader pays in cash — in the shop, or to our courier. No cards, no wallets, no escrow.
The courier is oursEmployed, not contracted. The cash they carry is the company’s float, not a stranger’s promise — which is what makes a cash model workable at all.
Free first month, then 12%The 30 days start at a seller’s first offer, not at sign-up. After that, 12% of what they sold here falls due each month.
Access is the only leverSince the money is cash, the platform cannot withhold it. An unpaid seller keeps their orders but stops receiving new demand — except the requests nobody in good standing answered.
Thin but completeShip the whole loop — request, offer, order, review — before adding depth anywhere in it.

User stories

Eight epics in build order. Each story carries acceptance criteria and the screens it lives on — click a screen code to jump to its wireframe.

Filter
E1

Accounts & identity

4 stories
US-01

As a reader, I want to be let in with one tap and asked for nothing else, so that I can post the book I came here for before the app starts asking who I am.

  • Sign-up is Google, and that is the whole screen. No name, no phone, no address, no confirmation mail. The account exists the moment Google answers.
  • Nothing personal is asked until there is an order to put it on. Name, phone and address are taken at checkout (US-21), and only then — a reader who posts a request and never buys is never asked for them. At checkout the reader can see why an address is wanted; on a sign-up screen it reads as a toll.
  • The role is fixed by the door the person came through; one account = one role in the MVP.
  • After sign-up the reader lands on My requests, empty, with one button: Ask for a book. Readers arrive from adverts and social links, so the first screen after the tap has to be the thing they were promised.
ReaderS-01S-02
US-02

As a bookseller, I want to give the shop’s details once, during registration, so that the first request I answer already carries a name, a photo and an address a reader can walk to.

  • Registration is a four-step wizard, and none of it is optional: the shop — name, photo, one line about it; the first branch — label, street, opening hours, pickup on or off; how the shop is paid and who the office writes to about the statement; then a review screen and Open the shop. A reader is about to be asked to hand cash to this shop; the shop can be asked for four screens.
  • This is the opposite of the reader’s door, on purpose. A reader with no name is still a reader; a shop with no name, address or hours cannot be offered to anybody, so there is nothing worth deferring.
  • Required: shop or seller name, avatar or shop photo, and at least one location — registration cannot complete without one.
  • A location is: label (“Mashtots branch”, “Warehouse”, “Home stock”), street address, city, opening hours, and whether buyers may pick up there.
  • Optional after the fact: a longer bio, more branches, a different photo. The shop does not set delivery methods or a courier price — delivery is the platform’s, one fee per order (US-39).
  • The profile is public at a stable URL, lists every location, and is reachable from every offer card.
  • A seller with no completed orders shows a New seller badge — never an invented rating.
BooksellerS-01S-17
US-03

As any user, I want to edit my profile and delivery details, so that my orders go to the right place.

  • Reader profile holds name, phone, default delivery address and city.
  • Seller profile holds shop details plus a list of locations: add, rename, edit hours, mark pickup on or off, and retire one.
  • A location with active offers cannot be deleted, only retired — the offers must first be moved to another location or removed.
  • Changes apply to future orders only; placed orders keep the details captured at checkout.
  • Appearance is on the same screen for all three roles: Light · Dark · Match my phone, defaulting to the phone. Every colour in the app is a token that flips with it, including the reds, so nothing has to be checked twice. A bookseller working under shop lights and a courier outdoors at midday both want light; forcing a reader's night setting on them is not a preference we get to make for them.
BothS-17
US-40

As the office, I want to create a courier’s account myself and hand them a login, so that only people we have actually hired can see a reader’s address and carry our cash.

  • There is no courier sign-up. No door, no form, no invite link a stranger can follow. An operator creates the account from the ops side — name, phone, employee number — and the system issues a login and a first password, handed over in person.
  • The courier signs in through a third door on the sign-in screen (Reader · Bookseller · Courier), with login and password rather than Google, and is required to change the password on first sign-in.
  • The reason is not ceremony. A courier sees home addresses, phone numbers, gate codes and the cash on every order in the city. That is the one role where self-service registration would be an unlocked door, and it is also the only role we can vouch for — they are staff, on the payroll, with an employee number.
  • An operator can deactivate an account in one action; the courier is signed out, keeps no access, and any cash still recorded against them (US-38) stays on the books until it is settled.
  • One account per person. Couriers are not readers or sellers on the same login, and nothing in the app offers to turn them into one.

E2

Posting a request

4 stories
US-04

As a reader, I want to post an open request in plain words — “I’m wondering about a book with a crazy scientist” — so that I can ask for a book I cannot name.

  • Description is the only required field; minimum 10 characters, maximum 1000.
  • No title, author or ISBN is demanded; the request headline is the first line of the description.
  • The request is published to the seller feed immediately, marked OPEN, and stays open until the reader orders or closes it — there is no deadline.
  • The reader may attach up to 3 reference images (a cover they half-remember, a photo of a shelf).
ReaderS-03S-04
US-05

As a reader, I want to post a concrete request with title, author and edition, so that sellers know exactly what to look for.

  • A two-way toggle at the top of the form switches between Something specific and Something like….
  • Specific mode requires at least a title or an ISBN; author, year, edition, language are optional.
  • Request kind is stored and shown as a badge on the card so sellers can filter by it.
ReaderS-03
US-06

As a reader, I want to set a budget, condition and delivery city on my request, so that I do not get offers I cannot use.

  • Optional fields: max price, condition (any / new / good used / any used), language, delivery city, delivery vs pickup.
  • No deadline field. A request runs until it is answered or the reader closes it, so a hard-to-find book can sit in the feed for months and still get an offer.
  • Filters are shown to sellers as chips on the request card but never block them from offering.
ReaderS-03S-13
US-07

As a reader, I want to see all my requests with their status and offer count, and close any of them myself, so that I stay in charge of what is still being hunted for me.

  • Request cards show status, offer count, an unseen-offers badge and how long the request has been open.
  • A request can be edited only until the first offer arrives; after that only closing is allowed.
  • Closing by hand removes it from the seller feed and marks every open offer on it Done · not chosen; the offers stay visible to the reader.
  • A request with no offers at all after 30 days moves to an Archive section: out of the seller feed, still in the reader’s list, back in play with one tap.
  • Archiving is silent and reversible, and only ever touches requests that nobody answered — a request with offers on it stays open until the reader acts.
  • A fulfilled, closed or archived request can be reposted as a fresh copy in one tap — useful when the book arrived damaged or the reader wants another copy.

E3

Finding requests to answer

3 stories
US-08

As a bookseller, I want a feed of request cards, newest first, so that I can skim live demand between customers.

  • Each card shows the request text (3 lines max), kind badge, budget, city, age and current offer count.
  • A request I have answered leaves the feed. It is not demand any more — it is waiting on a reader, and that waiting belongs in My offers, grouped by the request it answers. Dimming it in place only made the seller read the same title twice a day to remember they had already dealt with it.
  • The feed is therefore a to-do list: everything on it is something nobody at this shop has replied to. Emptying it is an achievement, and the empty state says so and points at My offers.
  • Answering again is not lost, only moved: My offers → the request → Offer another book, up to the ten-book cap (US-13). Back from there returns to My offers, not to the feed.
  • Infinite scroll or paging; the feed also excludes closed, archived and restricted-hidden requests.
BooksellerS-12
US-09

As a bookseller, I want to filter and search the feed, so that I only see what my shelves can answer.

  • Filters: keyword, city, budget range, request kind, language. There is no unanswered only filter, because after US-08 the whole feed is unanswered. City matching uses the seller’s locations, so a shop with two branches sees requests near either.
  • Filter state survives navigating into a request and back.
  • An empty result offers to clear filters or save the keyword as an alert.
BooksellerS-12
US-10

As a bookseller, I want to be notified when a request matching my keywords appears, so that I can answer first.

  • Up to 5 saved keyword alerts per seller, editable from the feed.
  • Matching request triggers an in-app notification and an optional email, at most one digest per hour.
  • The notification deep-links to the request detail.
BooksellerS-12S-13
E4

Answering with books

4 stories
US-11

As a bookseller, I want to answer a request with a book I actually have — photos, title, condition, price and my own note — so that I can present it as well as I would in the shop.

  • The form is short screens, never one long one. Step one is the book: title, author, year, photos. Step two is condition, price and branch. Publisher, edition, language, pages, binding and the optional note sit behind More about this copy, on their own screen, so the required path stays two taps deep and every screen's button is reachable without scrolling.
  • Required: at least one photo of the real copy, title, price, condition, and which location the book is at. Optional: author, year, publisher, edition, language, pages.
  • The note is optional, always. Two things are required of a book and only two: what it is, and what it costs. A bookseller with thirty copies to list should not be blocked by a writing task, and a note squeezed out under protest is filler that helps nobody.
  • On an open-ended request it is encouraged rather than demanded, by giving it a screen of its own with one field on it and a line saying why it matters: the reader did not name a book, so this is the only thing separating two copies from a list of prices. Persuasion, not a gate.
  • On an open-ended request the note is the first field on step two, above condition and price, with the request quoted over it and a placeholder that shows the shape of a good answer. On a specific request it stays optional and sits where it always did, framed as “your note about this copy”.
  • The note travels to the reader, not just to the detail page: see US-15.
  • On an open-ended request the note gets a screen to itself — the book on one screen, why it answers on the next, condition and price on the third. It is the field the whole offer turns on, and sharing a screen with a price box is how it becomes an afterthought. The screen can be walked past with the field empty.
  • A seller with one location has it filled in automatically and never sees the field; a seller with several must choose, and the field cannot be left on a guess.
  • The location travels with the offer: the offer card shows the branch and its city, the detail shows the address and pickup hours.
  • Up to 5 photos, drag to reorder, first one is the cover shown on the card.
  • Photos upload from camera or file; upload progress and failure are visible per photo.
  • On send, the offer appears on the reader’s request within seconds and the reader is notified.
BooksellerS-13S-14
US-12

As a bookseller, I want to send several books to one request, so that an open request gets a small shortlist from me.

  • One offer is one book in the record — three books are three offers, each with its own photos, price and status, so one can sell while another is declined.
  • They are composed together, though. The form ends on two buttons: Send the offer and Add another book. Adding another keeps the finished book in a basket at the top of the form and opens a blank one; nothing reaches the reader until Send. So a seller who finds three candidates on the shelf sends one offer of three books, and the reader gets one notification, not three.
  • The basket is visible while composing — cover, title, condition, price, and Remove on each — and the header counts the book being filled in (“Book 3 of this offer”).
  • Every book in the basket stays editable until the offer goes. Each carries Edit, which swaps it with the one being filled so neither is lost, and Remove. A basket line missing a title or a price says so in place rather than failing silently at the end.
  • Back never leaves the flow while there is unsent work. From the first screen of book 3 it returns to book 2 — labelled with that book's number, not a bare arrow. Only with an empty basket does back leave for the request.
  • Send validates every book, not just the one on screen. If an earlier one is short of a title or a price, Send opens that book at that screen with the message on it, and parks the current one in its place.
  • Leaving the flow entirely before sending loses the basket. It is a composing state, not a draft; nothing is stored server-side until Send.
  • A configurable cap limits how many offers one seller may put on one request — set to 10 for now. It is a platform setting, changed without a release, not a constant in the code.
  • At the cap the button is disabled and says so: “you have 10 books on this request — remove one to add another”.
  • The cap exists to keep the reader’s list readable, not to ration sellers; watch it in the first months and raise or lower it on evidence.
BooksellerS-14S-15
US-13

As a bookseller, I want to see what happened to every offer I sent, so that I know what sells and what is ignored.

  • Offer list grouped by status: active (sent, seen, in a cart) and closed (done · sold, done · not chosen, declined, removed).
  • An active offer has no clock on it — it stays live until the reader orders, so an old offer is still a live one.
  • Each row links back to the request and forward to the order when there is one.
  • Counters at the top: offers sent this week, sold, conversion.
BooksellerS-15
US-14

As a bookseller, I want to keep editing an offer, and to remove it when I sell the book in the shop, so that what the reader sees is always what is actually on my shelf.

  • Edit anything while the offer is active — photos, title, all book fields, condition, price, note, location — using the same form that created it.
  • Remove is available for any offer not yet ordered, with a reason: sold in the shop, can’t find it, changed my mind. Since nothing expires, removing is the only way an offer leaves the reader’s list.
  • A removed offer disappears from the reader immediately. It is not erased: it stays in the seller’s closed list and in the record, because it may already sit in a cart or a statement.
  • An offer that is part of an order cannot be edited or removed at all — that copy is promised.
  • If the offer sits in a cart when it changes, the reader sees it flagged in place: “price updated” or “condition updated”, with the old value struck through, and must re-confirm it at checkout.
  • If it is removed while in a cart, the item is flagged no longer available and blocks checkout until the reader takes it out.
  • One copy, several requests. A shop with one Frankenstein may well have offered it to three different requests. Those are three separate offers, and when one of them sells the other two are promises the seller can no longer keep.
  • So the moment a sale is confirmed, the seller is shown their other active offers and asked, in one tap each: “did this order use the same copy? remove the others.” Not automatic — only the seller knows whether they had two copies.
  • The same prompt appears when a book is sold over the counter to a walk-in customer: “sold in the shop” is the first remove reason for exactly this reason.
  • No stale-offer reminders in v1 — an old offer is assumed live until the seller edits or removes it.

E5

Comparing offers & trusting the seller

4 stories
US-15

As a reader, I want the offers on my request laid out as cards with photo, price, condition and who is selling, so that I can compare at a glance.

  • Card: cover photo, title + author, condition chip, price, seller name, branch and city, star rating, review count, “New seller” badge when applicable.
  • The seller's note is on the card, not only in the detail. On an open-ended request it leads — set under the title in the serif face, clamped to four lines, before author and condition — because it is the thing being compared. On a specific request it sits under the author line, quieter, clamped to three.
  • When a seller has several branches the card names the one holding this copy, so two offers from the same shop are never confused.
  • Sort by newest, price low→high, rating; new offers since last visit are marked.
  • Add to cart works straight from the card, without opening the detail.
ReaderS-04
US-16

As a reader, I want to open an offer and see every photo and detail the seller gave, so that I can judge the actual copy.

  • Photo gallery, full description, all book fields the seller filled, seller note, price, and the exact location: branch label, address, pickup hours, map link.
  • Condition is shown with a plain-language explanation of what that grade means.
  • Primary action is Add to cart; secondary actions are Decline and Open seller.
ReaderS-05
US-17

As a reader, I want to open the seller’s profile and read what other buyers said, so that I can decide whether to trust the sale.

  • Profile shows average rating, number of ratings, completed orders, member since, bio and all locations with their addresses and pickup hours.
  • Recent buyer comments with star value, first name and date; newest first, paged.
  • The seller’s other open offers to my requests are listed so I can combine them into one order.
ReaderS-06
US-18

As a reader, I want to decline offers that miss the point, so that my list stays short and the seller gets a signal.

  • Decline hides the offer behind a “declined (n)” collapsed group and can be undone.
  • Optional one-tap reason: wrong book, too expensive, condition, too far.
  • The seller sees the status and the reason, aggregated, never as a message.
ReaderS-04S-05
E6

Cart & ordering

4 stories
US-19

As a reader, I want to collect books from different requests and different sellers in one cart, so that I can order everything at once.

  • Cart holds offers, not catalogue items; an offer can be in the cart of its reader only.
  • Cart badge shows item count and survives sign-out and device change (server-side cart).
  • Adding an offer already in the cart is a no-op with a “already in cart” hint.
ReaderS-04S-07
US-20

As a reader, I want my cart grouped by bookseller and branch, with one delivery choice underneath it, so that I know which shop each book comes from and what the whole order costs to get to me.

  • One group per seller location: items, fee, subtotal. Two books from the same shop but different branches are two groups, and the group header says which branch.
  • Delivery is one choice for the whole order, not one per shop. A reader either has the order brought to them or goes out to collect it; an order arriving two different ways is a thing nobody asked for and a bill nobody can check. The control sits once, under the groups, above the total.
  • Pickup is offered only when every shop in the cart allows it. One warehouse with no counter and the whole order goes by courier — the cart says which shop and why, rather than disabling a button with no explanation.
  • One delivery fee for the order, whatever is in it. Two books cost the same as one; two shops cost the same as one. The reader is being charged for a delivery — one trip to one door — and the collecting is our logistics problem, not a line on their bill. The copy says so: “a courier collects from 2 shops and brings everything to you — 1 000 ֏, one delivery”.
  • The fee is the platform’s, not the shop’s. Booksellers do not set a courier price and are not paid out of it; they are paid for books (US-39). A shop that never touches the fee can never be blamed for it, and two shops in one cart cannot quietly double it.
  • Pickup means no fee at all: the reader collects from each shop’s counter and pays that shop there.
  • Remove item; empty group disappears; empty cart shows an empty state back to my requests.
  • Removed offers are flagged in place and blocked from checkout; edited ones are flagged “updated by the seller” and need one tap to accept.
ReaderS-07
US-21

As a reader, I want a checkout that takes my delivery details and shows what I will owe in cash, so that I can finish the purchase and turn up with the right money.

  • Cart → checkout → placed. Checkout is one page that reads like an invoice: who it goes to, the books grouped by bookseller, the delivery line, the total, the button. There is no separate review step and no payment step — payment is cash on handover. The details form is the one thing that can step aside onto its own screen, because four fields and an invoice do not share a phone (below).

First-timers, and anyone whose details are incomplete, get the same block already open as a form, and the approve button stays disabled until it is filled.

There is no Confirmed state. Rules worth writing down

Integrity

  • An offer belongs to exactly one request and exactly one location. The same physical copy may be offered to several requests as several offers — the platform cannot tell, and the seller is responsible for removing the rest once it sells.
  • The cap on offers per seller per request is a platform setting (10 today), not a constant. Same for the commission rate.
  • Offers and requests have no clock. The only terminal events are an order, a decline, a withdrawal, or the reader closing the request.
  • Placing an order is atomic: ordered offers → done·sold, every other open offer on those requests → done·not chosen, requests → fulfilled. Cancelling reverses all three.
  • Ordering freezes the offer into an OrderItem; later edits to the offer never change a placed order.
  • A seller cannot see other sellers’ prices on a request, only the offer count.
  • The courier note is the courier's, not the order's. It goes to the reader who wrote it and to the courier carrying the job. The bookseller never sees it, at any status, on any screen — a gate code is a delivery instruction, not an order attribute. The field says so where it is typed.
  • Reader identity is limited to first name and city until the sub-order is marked ready. The phone and the address are released at that moment and not before — a shop that has not committed a copy gets nothing.
  • Reversal is exact, not approximate. An offer released by order 1042 carries closed_by_order_id = 1042, so cancelling 1042 brings back those offers and no others — not every not-chosen offer on the request, some of which a different order closed.
  • A cancelled sub-order always says who cancelled it and why, from a fixed list. “Cancelled” with no reason is not a state we allow to exist.
  • Money owed is not money held. A refund the office has promised the courier is a pending cash entry: it shows as a debt on both screens and only moves into the courier's hands when someone marks it received.

Reputation maths

  • Rating = mean stars over completed sub-orders, shown with the count; hidden below 3 reviews, replaced by “New seller”.
  • Completed orders counter is public; declines and auto-cancels are internal only in the MVP.
  • Reviews are immutable after 24 hours and cannot be removed by the seller.

Commission maths

  • Base = sum of OrderItem prices in sub-orders completed during the period. Delivery fees are never part of the base.
  • Commission = rate × base, computed per line and summed, so a disputed line can be lifted out cleanly.
  • Sub-orders completed during the trial are stamped trial and stay out of every statement.
  • Restriction is derived from standing, not stored per request; the fallback pool is a query — “no offer from a good-standing seller, posted more than 72 h ago”.
  • The silence ladder is time plus a zero offer count, so it needs no state of its own: at 24 h a note, at 72 h the wider pool, at 30 days archived.

Scope & open decisions

What this MVP deliberately does not do, and the choices that are now settled and safe to build against.

Out of scope for v1

  • Messaging between buyer and seller. Everything a buyer needs must fit on the offer card and the profile — that constraint is the product, not a gap.
  • Counter-offers, haggling, auctions.
  • Seller inventory or catalogue browsing; there is nothing to search, only requests to answer.
  • Any online payment at all — cards, wallets, escrow, refunds, returns. Books are paid in cash; commission is settled offline.
  • Automatic collection of commission, invoices as legal documents, tax reporting, per-seller contracts in-app.
  • Shipping-carrier integration and live tracking numbers.
  • Buyer ratings, seller replies to reviews, review moderation queue.
  • One account holding both roles; wishlists; saved sellers; follow.
  • An admin panel. The ops page (S-20) is the entire back office; everything else is a database query.
  • Moving a book between a seller’s own branches to combine two handovers into one.
  • Mobile apps — responsive web only.

Settled — build to these

  • Rate: 12% of the books total, delivery excluded. Stored per seller account, so one shop or everyone can be changed without a release.
  • Free month starts at the first sent offer. An account that sits idle has not used anything yet.
  • Each shop bills on its own anniversary day, due the day it is issued, reminded the day before. Unpaid that night → restricted; paying lifts it at once. Nothing escalates past restriction automatically — a second unpaid month is a phone call.
  • One open hour to confirm an order, counted only during that branch's opening hours.
  • Three days to collect a pickup, reminded on day one and day two, then the shop releases it in one tap and the request reopens.
  • Silence ladder: 24 h / 72 h / 30 days. Reader hears “still looking” at a day; the request opens to every seller at three days; with still no offers it archives at a month, reversible in one tap.
  • No stale-offer nudges. An old offer is live until the seller withdraws it.
  • Two branches, two handovers. No transferring a book between a seller’s own locations in v1.
  • One offer is one book, and a seller may put up to 10 on a single request — a platform setting, not a constant, so it moves on evidence.
  • Offers stay editable and removable for their whole active life. Removing asks why; “sold in the shop” is the common one. After a sale the seller is prompted to clear their other offers of the same copy.
  • One city. Yerevan only — no city field anywhere. It comes back the day a second city opens.
  • A courier is a third role, and an employee — two taps, collected and delivered. Delivery completes the order; the shop’s job ends at “ready for the courier”.
  • Both settlement directions ship in v1. Settle after is preferred and is the default; pay at pickup exists for shops that want cash on the spot. Per shop, overridable per job.
  • The code is for pickups only. A delivery is closed by the courier, or by the reader — a courier already knows whose door he knocked on.
  • One ops page instead of an admin panel: statements, mark paid, resolve a flagged line, override a rate or standing. Nothing else.
  • Every threshold lives in one settings table: offer cap, rate, 24 h, 72 h, 30 d, 1 open hour, 3-day pickup window.

Built for a phone

Three of the four people in this product are standing up when they use it — a bookseller behind a counter, a courier on a street, a reader on a bus. The rules below are measured, not judged by eye, and every screen in the prototype is checked against them.

No form is taller than one screen

At 390×844 — the phone we design to — the primary button must end above 690px, so it is visible without scrolling while the keyboard is down. That is a budget, and the parts cost what they cost:

PartHeight
Heading and its one line of help~135px
A labelled field~78px
A textarea~115px
The button40px

Which makes the ceiling four fields and a heading. A fifth field is not a design decision, it is a second screen. This is why the request form, the offer form and the checkout details are steps rather than pages — not because stepping is fashionable, but because the arithmetic leaves no choice.

Everything you tap is at least 36px

  • Buttons, back links, segment controls, toggles and the small remove-chips all reach 36px in their smallest state — back links and toggles get the height from padding, so nothing grows visually.
  • No screen scrolls sideways at 390px. Long titles truncate; long numbers wrap.
  • Nothing relies on text under 12px. Decorative type on a book cover is the one exception, and it is marked aria-hidden so it is never read out either.
  • Repeated information is cut wherever a row already carries it: an invoice names a shop once, an order names the reader once, a job names the address once.
  • All of it is checked by a script across every screen in all three roles — overflow, small type, small targets, duplicated text — rather than by opening the app and looking.
