The Supply Test

Before a line of code: find out whether booksellers actually answer requests. Run the whole loop by hand on WhatsApp for two weeks — you are the platform — and come out with one number that decides whether M1 gets built.

10 shops 30 real requests No app No commission Decides M1
01

Why this comes before the build

Will a second-hand bookseller, unpaid and unprompted, answer a stranger’s request with a photo and a price — within a day, more than once?

Everything in the spec assumes yes. Twenty screens, four state machines and a commission model all sit on top of that one behaviour. If the answer is no, you have not built the wrong feature — you have built the wrong product.

It needs no appA request is a message. An offer is a photo and two lines. WhatsApp already does both — the app only makes it scale.
It costs two weeksAgainst roughly three months for M1. The worst outcome is learning in week twelve what week two would have told you.
It produces launch supplyThe shops that answer by hand are the shops that make day one of the real app non-empty.
It tests the no-chat ruleEvery question a shop has to ask you before they can quote is either a missing field on the offer form, or proof the rule breaks.
The one thing that invalidates the test: inventing the requests yourself. Fake asks get polite answers and teach you nothing about speed or effort. Every request must come from a person who actually wants the book and will actually pay for it.
02

The two weeks

Two tracks run in parallel for the first four days — shops and readers — then the loop runs for ten. Budget about an hour a day, more on the recruiting days.

Days 1–4 · track A

Recruit 10 shops, in person

Second-hand booksellers do not sign up through a form. Walk in, ask for the owner, use the pitch in §04. Expect six yeses from ten visits.

  • Bring nothing to sign. The ask is: “reply to book requests on WhatsApp for two weeks.”
  • Take their WhatsApp number and the name they want shown to buyers.
  • Say plainly that you are running it by hand and that nothing is charged during the test.
  • Prefer variety over volume: two general second-hand shops, one antiquarian, one specialist, one stall, one online-only reseller. You are testing whether the model works across shop types.
Days 1–4 · track B

Collect 30 real requests

You need demand that is genuinely someone’s. Book clubs, university reading groups, Facebook and Telegram book groups, friends who read, the readers who already complain that a title is impossible to find.

  • Ask for it in their own words — do not tidy it into a catalogue entry.
  • Aim for a spread: roughly half concrete (title, author, edition) and half open (“something about a crazy scientist”). The open half is the part nobody else in the market serves.
  • Record their budget and city, and confirm they will actually buy if the right copy appears.
  • Thirty is the target; twenty is the floor. Below twenty the percentages mean nothing.
Days 5–14

Run the loop by hand

Release requests in a steady trickle — three or four a day, not thirty on Monday. A flood teaches you nothing about a normal day behind the counter.

  • Send each request to every shop individually, never to a group. Group chats create herd behaviour and hide who ignored what.
  • Forward offers to the reader exactly as the shop sent them. Do not improve the photo, fix the price or rewrite the description — you are measuring what an unassisted offer looks like.
  • Wait the full 72 hours before deciding a request got no answer.
  • Let the sales actually happen: reader goes to the shop, pays cash, takes the book. A test that stops before money changes hands has not tested anything.
  • Log every question a shop asks you. This list is the most valuable thing the test produces.
Day 15

Read the numbers against the gate

The thresholds in §03 were set before the test started, on purpose. Compare, decide, and do not renegotiate them after seeing the result.

03

Go / no-go, decided in advance

Write these down now, while you have no data and nothing to defend. The point of a threshold set beforehand is that it can still say no to you afterwards.

Build M1

All four hold

  • ≥ 60% of requests get at least one offer within 72 hours
  • Median time to first offer under 24 hours
  • ≥ 40% of the open requests get an offer — the vague ones, not just the ones with an ISBN
  • ≥ 4 shops answered more than three times each
The last one matters most. One polite reply is courtesy; repeat answering is supply.
Change something first

The partial results

  • Offers arrive, but only from one or two shops → you have a supplier, not a marketplace. Recruit differently before building.
  • Only concrete requests get answered → the open half needs a different mechanic, or the positioning changes.
  • Offers arrive but nothing sells → the problem is price, condition or trust, not supply. Watch why readers said no.
  • Almost every offer needed a follow-up question → the no-chat rule is wrong, or the offer form is missing fields.
Each of these is a fixable spec change, not a dead end — but fix it before writing code, not after.
Stop and rethink

The hard no

  • Under 30% of requests answered in 72 hours, even after you personally chased the shops
  • Shops answer for the first three days, then go quiet — novelty, not demand
Then the product is a different one: seeded supply you buy yourself, a catalogue you build for the shops, or a paid answer. All of those are worth considering — none of them is the app in the spec.
04

What to say in the shop

Sixty seconds, standing at the counter, no laptop. Lead with their shelves, not with your platform.

The openingsay it out loud
People are looking for books you already have on your shelves — they just
can't find you. I collect what they're asking for and bring it to you.
You reply with a photo and a price, or you ignore it.

No listing. No inventory. No computer work. One photo and two lines.
The money, when they askdo not bury this
Free for your first month — that starts when you send your first offer,
not today.

After that it's 12% of what you actually sell through it. Only what you
sell. If you sell nothing, you owe nothing.

The buyer pays you in cash, in your shop or to the courier, the way you
work now. I never touch the money.

The five objections, and what is actually true

“I don’t have time for this.”

Answer only the ones you can answer in thirty seconds. Ignoring a request costs nothing and counts against nothing — there is no response rate, no penalty, no rating for silence.

“12% is a lot.”

It is on a sale you would not otherwise have had. This is not a browser who wandered in — it is someone who described exactly this book and is waiting for it. And it is charged after the sale, not before.

“Why not just put my books on the sites that already exist?”

Because that means listing four thousand books and keeping them current. Here the buyer does the describing and you answer only when you happen to have it. The work is the opposite way round.

“How do I know it’s worth it?”

You will see, during these two weeks, exactly how many requests come per week and how many you could have filled. Nothing is charged and nothing is signed. If it is not worth it, you stop answering.

“Who pays the delivery?”

The buyer. Or they collect it from your shop, which puts them in front of your shelves — which is usually the better outcome for you.

Be honest that it is a test. “For now I’m doing this by hand on WhatsApp — if it works, it becomes an app and you’ll be the first shops on it.” Shops forgive a scrappy test. They do not forgive discovering they were a test.

06

What to write down

Two tables in a spreadsheet, filled in as you go. Reconstructing this at the end from a WhatsApp scroll is how tests end up proving whatever you already believed.

One row per request

ColumnWhy it is there
id, text, kind (open/concrete)The open/concrete split is the headline result — it says whether the vague half of the product works.
posted_at, first_offer_atMedian time to first offer. The single most predictive number you will collect.
offers_24h, offers_72hTests the 24 h and 72 h thresholds already written into the spec.
shops_answeredNames, not a count — it reveals whether supply is a market or one keen shop.
photo_okWas the photo usable as the card’s cover image? A shelf photo taken in bad light is a product problem.
bought, why_notSeparates a supply failure from a price, condition or trust failure.
questions_askedEvery clarification a shop needed before quoting. Each one is a field to add or a rule to reconsider.

One row per shop

ColumnWhy it is there
name, type, locationsType (general, antiquarian, specialist, stall, online) shows which kind of shop the model fits.
said_yes, visited_atYour conversion rate on the doorstep, which is also your future recruitment cost.
requests_seen, offers_madeAnswer rate per shop. Under 10% means they said yes to be polite.
median_responseSome shops answer in ten minutes, some in two days. It decides whether 72 hours is the right window.
sold, valueWhat 12% would have been. Shows a shop what they left on the table — the strongest thing you can say at launch.
still_answering_wk2The novelty check. Week-two silence is the result that matters most.

The qualitative log

A plain list, one line per incident, kept for the whole two weeks: every question a shop asked, every request a reader phrased in a way you had to interpret, every moment you were tempted to help a shop write a better offer. This list is what turns the spec from a guess into a design.

Do not measure

  • Anything about the interface — there isn’t one.
  • Willingness to pay commission. Nobody is being charged, so any answer is polite fiction.
  • Reader retention. Two weeks is too short to mean anything.
07

The day after

If the gate says go

  • Build M1 — sign-up, requests, feed, offers with photos. Nothing about money.
  • Onboard the shops that answered, by hand, before launch day. Their WhatsApp offers become the app’s first real content.
  • Show each shop their own line: requests seen, offers made, sold, value. That is the recruitment pitch for every shop after them.
  • Feed the questions log straight into the offer form (S-14) and the request form (S-03) before building either.
  • Start the free month properly at their first offer in the app — the test does not consume it.

Two things to write while it runs

  • The seller agreement: one page, plain language — 12%, what is owed when, that restriction is the consequence of not paying. The commission rests on trust and feed access, so it should be something a seller actually agreed to.
  • The instrumentation list for M1, taken from the columns above. Time to first offer, share of requests answered in 72 h, offers per request, order rate, repeat-request rate. Build them in from the first release — they are the same numbers, measured automatically instead of by hand.
The result you should hope for is a mixed one. A clean pass tells you only that you were right. The partial results in §03 are the ones that change the product while changing it is still cheap.
